EricJhonsa (@EricJhonsa)

关于我在 @TomorrowXSummit 上的所见所闻，几点想法：
1) 正如许多人已经指出的，会上机构投资者对 AI 交易普遍持谨慎和看空态度（科技从业者和个人投资者往往更乐观）。不过，虽然我听到了一大堆看空论点，但考虑到当前估值已经计入了大幅的资本开支削减，我认为只有两个论点真正站得住脚：
A) 近期对 $ANT 推理收入和算力的估算表明，我们需要的算力（GW）将远少于目前计划建设的规模。
B) 前沿实验室 ARR 增速近期放缓，表明产生的收入将不足以支撑计划中的资本开支。
我认为第一个论点往往依赖可疑的数字——例如，认为 Anthropic 只需 500MW 推理容量就能产生 1000 亿美元收入，而实际上他们今天可能正在用 1.2–1.3GW 来实现 800 亿美元的总 ARR。更重要的是，它忽略了 Anthropic 将超过 60% 的算力用于训练——以及为什么这个比例在 2027 年可能不会下降多少，甚至完全不降——也忽略了为什么竞争对手前沿实验室和开源权重模型的收入/推理 GW 数据可能大不相同（就 OpenAI 而言，它可能依靠超过 2GW 的推理算力来产生约 700 亿美元的总 ARR）。
在我看来第二个看空论点更有力，因为明年资本开支可能在 1.5 万亿美元左右，而编程相关的增长正在放缓。尽管如此，我认为执着于 OAI/Ant 第四季度 ARR 增速，忽略了以下几点：
A) 在计入开源权重模型和其他专有模型、科技巨头内部的 AI 使用、互联网公司和 ISV、本地 AI 部署等之后，OAI/Ant 似乎只占非中国 AI 算力消耗的 25–30%。
B) 开源权重模型的 token 消耗今年爆发得有多猛（从 OpenRouter、Fireworks 等的数据来看，同比增长远超 10 倍）。
C) 许多非编程工作负载（知识/企业工作、网络安全、研发等）的采用曲线似乎还处于多么早期的阶段。
D) OAI/Ant 的算力承诺、资产负债表和大模型进展，使它们不太可能因为一两个季度的 ARR 增长放缓而大幅改变资本开支计划。
E) 云巨头们每季度报告的内容以及对未来几年的信号（$AMZN 指引 2027 年和 2028 年 AWS 增速均超过 40%，而此前它从未给出超过一个季度的指引，这一点尤其值得注意）。
F) 过去的资本开支热潮（铁路、工厂、光纤等）通常是在供应/产能远远超前于需求多年之后才结束，而不是在供应只是适度超前于需求之后就结束（当然，目前连这种情况都还没出现）。
与此同时，虽然我听到了大量看空论点，但我提出的各种看多论点（涉及 token/云增长、GPU 价格、未来非编程领域的采用、大模型进展等）受到的反驳比我预想的要少。再加上我听到和无意中听到的各种想回避 AI 基础设施的非基本面原因（例如想锁定全年收益、不想冒跑输同行/基准的风险等），让我觉得最近看到的很多悲观情绪，来自投资者被近期的价格走势吓到/搅乱，于是试图事后为这种走势找理由，就像今年早些时候 SaaS 和互联网股票那样。
2) 密切关注大模型进展的人对明年将出现重大突破的乐观程度令人瞩目。我听到多个预测称明年将实现 RSI（递归自我改进）（可以说各实验室已经实现了部分 RSI——参见 METR 最近关于 Anthropic 大模型进展速度的报告），以及能够在各类机器人任务/环境中完全泛化的大模型将在 2027 年出现。
对于通用机器人/人形机器人而言，硬件工程可能很快就会成为大规模部署的最大剩余障碍。我目前没有把机器人拐点纳入对半导体的预期，这类事情通常也不会一夜之间发生，但如果/当它真的发生，对内存、算力、功率半导体、传感器等显然会有重大影响。
3) 与科技/产品人士关于企业 AI 采用（无论是整体还是其中某部分）的交流让我认为，短期内采用率很可能起伏不定、参差不齐——这种起伏当然也符合 Ant/OAI 近期 ARR 增速放缓的情况。但他们也给出了对更长时间维度保持乐观的理由。
目前，相对少数的早期采用者/重度用户（在公司、部门/团队和个人层面）贡献了非编程 token 增长的很大一部分。而且我的印象是，相当多的公司（尤其是科技和金融以外的公司）仍处于试错阶段，试图弄清楚哪些 AI 项目有、哪些没有很强的投资回报，以及如何让 token 支出物有所值。
与此同时，我听到了一些有趣的案例：AI 被用于分析海量且频繁变化的数据集并从中发现洞见、加速/改进芯片设计、帮助办公室员工处理大得多的工作量等。AlphaSense 表示其 token 消耗年化已达 80 万亿（自年初以来增长 8 倍），而且这还是在其使用量严重偏向重度用户的情况下（据称其 SuperAnalyst 工具前 1% 用户消耗的 token 是中位用户的 28 倍）。
另外，我认为如果假设非编程领域的大量 token 使用会永远局限于少数早期采用者/重度用户，那将是个错误。一方面因为大模型和应用开发者非常清楚这个问题，正在努力提升普通用户的使用量；另一方面因为大模型最近在处理复杂的知识/企业任务方面进步很大——明年还会有更大的进展，尤其是如果 RSI 实现的话——这应该会为 IT 部门和重度用户全面自动化各种智能体任务和工作流铺平道路。
——
总的来说，这次会议强化了我的观点：如果可以把 AI 热潮中的市场走势与互联网泡沫时代相比，那么最近的抛售更可能被视为类似 1998 年夏秋（当时纳斯达克从 7 月中旬到 10 月初下跌超过 30%，伴随动量急剧反转、宏观担忧和基金爆仓，一度年内转跌，随后狂欢继续），而不是类似 2000 年秋天（那时狂欢彻底结束）。
话虽如此，就估值/财务状况、技术/生产力进步和企业支出增长而言，我认为 AI 热潮与互联网/电信热潮的共同点，可能不如与 80 年代末至 90 年代初中期的 PC 和客户端/服务器热潮多——当时“智能”（以 CPU MIPS 衡量）成本的持续下降，被用户增长加上更先进软件和工作负载的资源需求所抵消，而且绰绰有余。

原文：A few thoughts on what I saw and heard at the @TomorrowXSummit:
1) As many others have noted, cautious and bearish views on the AI trade abounded at the conference among institutional investors (tech people and individual investors were often more bullish). But while I heard a slew of bearish arguments, I thought only two really carried weight given the massive capex cuts priced in by current valuations:
A) That recent $ANT inference revenue and compute estimates suggest we’ll need far fewer GW of compute than what’s currently set to be built.
B) That the recent slowdown in frontier lab ARR growth suggests we won’t see enough revenue generated to justify planned capex.
I think the first argument often relies on questionable numbers – for example, a belief that Anthropic only needs 500MW in inference capacity to produce $100B in revenue, when they might actually be using 1.2-1.3GW today to achieve $80B in gross ARR. And more importantly, it glosses over Anthropic dedicating over 60% of its compute to training -- and why that number might not decline much, if at all, in 2027 -- and why revenue/inference GW numbers probably look very different for rival frontier labs and open-weight models (for its part, OpenAI might be relying on over 2GW in inference to produce ~$70B in gross ARR).
The second bear argument is stronger IMO given that capex might be around $1.5T next year and coding-related growth is slowing down. That said, I think fixating on Q4 OAI/Ant ARR growth overlooks:
A) How OAI/Ant appear to make up just 25-30% of non-China AI compute consumption, after accounting for open-weight and rival proprietary models, internal AI usage among the tech giants, Internet firms and ISVs, local AI deployments, etc.
B) How much (judging by data from OpenRouter, Fireworks, etc.) open-weight token consumption has exploded this year (looks like it’s up well over 10x Y/Y).
C) How early we appear to be in the adoption curve for many non-coding workloads (knowledge/enterprise work, cyber, R&D, etc.).
D) How OAI/Ant’s compute commitments, balance sheets and LLM progress make them unlikely to meaningfully change their capex plans in response to a quarter or two of slow ARR growth.
E) What the cloud giants are both reporting quarterly as well as signaling for the next couple of years ($AMZN guiding for 40%+ AWS growth in both 2027 and 2028, after having previously never guided more than a quarter out, seems especially notable).
F) How past capex booms (railroads, factories, fiber, etc.) typically ended only after years of supply/capacity being well ahead of demand, rather than after supply got just moderately ahead of demand (and of course, even that’s not the case right now).
Meanwhile, though I heard plenty of bearish arguments, I got less pushback than I thought I would for various bullish arguments (related to token/cloud growth, GPU prices, future non-coding adoption, LLM progress, etc.). That, together with all the non-fundamental reasons I heard and overheard for wanting to avoid AI infra (e.g. wanting to lock in gains for the year, not wanting to risk underperforming peers/benchmarks, etc.), made me think a lot of the negativity we’ve seen lately comes from investors being frightened/unsettled by recent price action and thus trying to rationalize that price action ex-post, much as we saw with SaaS and Internet stocks earlier this year.
2) The optimism from people closely monitoring LLM progress about major advances happening next year was notable. I heard multiple predictions that RSI would be achieved next year (one could argue the labs have already achieved partial RSI – see METR’s recent report on the pace of Anthropic LLM advances), as well as that LLMs capable of fully generalizing across robotics tasks/environments would arrive in 2027.
With general-purpose robots/humanoids, it looks like hardware engineering might soon be the biggest remaining obstacle to mass deployment. I’m not currently baking a robotics inflection into my expectations for semis, and stuff like this usually doesn’t transpire overnight, but if/when it does happen, there are obviously big implications for memory, compute, power semis, sensors, etc.
3) Talks with tech/product people about enterprise AI adoption (whether in aggregate or some part of it) made me think we’ll likely see choppy/uneven adoption rates in the short-term -- and such choppiness of course fits with Ant/OAI’s recent ARR growth slowdowns. But they also provided reasons to be optimistic about what happens over longer timeframes.
For now, a relatively small number of early adopters/power users (at the corporate, department/team and individual levels) are driving a huge % of non-coding token growth. And I get the impression that quite a few companies (esp. outside of tech and finance) are still in the trial-and-error stage, trying to figure out which AI projects do and don’t have strong ROIs and how to get the most bang for their buck from their token spend.
At the same time, I heard interesting anecdotes about AI being used to analyze/uncover insights from massive and frequently-changing datasets, accelerate/improve chip design, help office workers handle much larger workloads, etc. AlphaSense said its token consumption is now up to 80T annualized (up 8x since the start of the year), and that’s even though its usage skews heavily towards power users (the top 1% of users for their SuperAnalyst tool were said to consume 28x as many tokens as the median user).
Also, I think it’d be a mistake to assume heavy non-coding token usage remains limited to a small number of early adopters/power users forever. Both because LLM and app developers are well-aware of this issue and trying hard to drive higher usage among normies, and because LLMs have been getting much better lately at handling complex knowledge/enterprise tasks – with bigger advances due next year, especially if RSI arrives – which should pave the way for IT departments and power users fully automating various agentic tasks and workflows.
—
On the whole, the conference strengthened my view that – to the extent one can compare market action during the AI boom to that of the Dotcom era – the recent selloff is more likely to go down as something comparable to the summer/fall of 1998 (when the Nasdaq fell over 30% from mid-July to early October amid a sharp momentum reversal, macro worries and fund blowups, and briefly went red for the year, before the party resumed) than something comparable to the fall of 2000 (when the party ended of good).
That said, in terms of valuations/financials, tech/productivity advances and corporate spending growth, I think the AI boom might have less in common with the Internet/telco boom than with the PC and client/server boom of the late '80s and early/mid ‘90s, when steady declines in the cost of “intelligence” (as defined by CPU MIPS) were more than offset by a combo of user growth and the resource demands of more advanced software and workloads.

原文：Ray Dalio explains how market bubbles form:
"The bubble, the mechanics of it, is that people mistake a great new technology for a great investment."
"Everything depends on AI, right? The markets, the economy, everything depends on AI. And so that technology is volatile and very risky."
Dalio explains that when everyone believes in a new technology, investors often borrow money to gain exposure, driving up demand and creating enormous wealth.
The problem starts when investors need cash, whether to repay debt, cover higher interest costs or pay taxes. They're forced to sell assets, even if they still believe in the technology.
As selling accelerates, confidence turns into fear, investors pull back, and the same forces that drove prices higher begin working in reverse.
